    04 - Jobs

    ISRC: QT6FZ2609872

    To keep from collapsing under the pressure of office routine, the narrator escapes to an imagined sanctuary.

    Instruction

    ## Setup

**BASICS**
* **Style:** Organic Indie-Folk, Folk-Pop
* **Mood:** Intimate, melancholic, introspective

**INSTRUMENTATION**
* **Main:** Felt Piano, Finger-picked Guitar
* **Texture:** Cello, soft Brushes on Snare, light Heartbeat-pulse Kick.
* **Vocals:** Female, breathy, extreme close-up "ASMR" quality.
* **Production:** High-fidelity, warm analog saturation, spacious reverb on 
  instruments but dry on vocals for intimacy.

## Arrangement

We open with restraint: overlaying the claustrophobia of the office with 
delicate fingerpicked acoustic guitar. In Verse 1, the narrator describes the 
corporate status quo almost stoically, with sparse instrumentation.

The spoken interlude ("He needs a birthday [...] / something expensive [...]") 
is delivered in a dry, exhausted monotone, relaying the manager's directives. 
Verse 2 builds emotional momentum as the narrator confesses weariness at living 
under constant obedience.

This triggers an internal escape into the chorus—a sudden release, an emotional 
exhalation bathed in the golden-hour light of Canadian pine woods. The listener 
must feel the dreamlike allure of this sanctuary: a profound yearning for 
crackling hearth fires, wool blankets, and silence.

The bridge wrenches the narrator back into the fluorescent present. 
The rhythmic, mechanical thud of the jammed printer translates into a driving, 
percussive pulse. The narrator sings—never shouting—against the manager's voice 
echoing down the hall, summoning her cottage even as the office encroaches. 
The emotional peak lands on "as I reach for the latch," followed by a bittersweet 
detachment from corporate ambition ("... far away from the game").

After letting the instruments breathe briefly, we enter a reprise of the chorus. 
It distills the essence of the first chorus at peak dynamic density. The narrator 
finds space to breathe, reveling one final time in the imagined landscape.

The outro ("The list is done...") returns to spoken word as instrumentation 
decays. Following "and I'm still here," all instruments fall away completely. 
The final lines—"am I?", a soft sigh, and "Man, I need another job"—are whispered 
into absolute silence.

The track must captivate without lulling the listener into passive comfort. 
It is not an idle lament, but a quiet assertion of internal resilience—hence the 
necessity of an underlying heartbeat pulse.

Crucial: The lyrics follow an exacting narrative trajectory. Treat them as 
immutable: do not alter, duplicate, smooth over, or omit lines. The thematic 
message takes strict precedence over metric symmetry.

    Track Liner Notes

    After the initial concept for Masks proved unworkable—its baroque imagery and theatrical conventions felt too heavy-handed—I needed to reorient. I grounded the setting in accessible, everyday reality, using cottagecore tropes to coax the warm, organic acoustic texture I envisioned. The sonic palette needed to be tender and consoling to counterbalance the sharp critique in the lyrics (a delicate challenge when working with generative models, which naturally attempt to unify lyrical sentiment and musical mood).

    The theatrical mask was transformed into the mundane, repetitive tasks that define corporate labor.

    I supplied the model with the following structural fragments:

    The narrator is caught in a corporate hamster wheel, punctuating her work with spoken-word asides: "He needs the list, now," and "He needs a birthday present for his wife, something expensive, something that looks like he cares."

    No one in the office cares about her inner life; she is treated purely as a functional human resource. A professional smile is demanded, and she complies stoically, because complaining is framed as weakness. She longs for escape: while emails flood her inbox like a torrent, she dreams of a woodland cabin, playing guitar by a hearth fire, writing songs for herself. While her manager shouts in the corridor, she imagines hot chocolate, soft flannel, and unhurried silence.

    The song concludes with a spoken reflection: "The list is done / the gift is wrapped / and I'm still here... Am I? / Man, I need another job."

    Having the narrator deliver the line "He needs the list, now" herself—rather than introducing a second voice for the manager—was a direct solution to audio generation constraints: Earlier tests trying to render multiple speakers resulted in the model either ignoring the dialogue shift or misinterpreting instructions like "muffled through an office telephone" as deafening, unlistenable distortion. Filtering the manager's commands through the narrator's own dry delivery worked far better: it positioned the boss as an alienated phantom, unable to penetrate the narrator's core identity.

    The initial draft was strong, but far too expansive for a 180-second ceiling. I loved the imagery of weaving spreadsheets on a loom and the phrase "ribbon-wrap the lie." However, test iterations proved that the lines were overly congested with syllables. To meet the time limit, I refactored the scansion and pruned secondary verses. "Fluorescent hum" was sacrificed, and "starving heart" (which risked reading as an unrequited office crush on the manager) was sharpened to "starving for soul."

    While the revised draft met the duration constraint, the harmonic tone felt wrong. I engaged in deeper prompt engineering, establishing an elaborate arrangement section that detailed the emotional progression of each segment. I needed to steer the model away from its default acoustic trajectory. This intervention proved decisive: the sonic texture softened into the comforting warmth I wanted, though generations remained somewhat structurally unstable.

    Suspecting that launching directly into non-melodic spoken word confused the audio engine, I queried Gemini, which confirmed the hypothesis: without an initial instrumental bar, the model struggled to anchor its rhythmic and harmonic pattern. Whether this is an architectural constraint or a conversational hallucination in response to a leading question remains debatable. The reality was that removing the spoken intro allowed me to reach the winning take within three iterations.

    Although that take omitted the intro and parts of the outro, I was able to splice those missing elements together from alternate iterations in post-production. What elevated the final take was its suspended felt piano chords and delicate tonal color. Combined with the vulnerable vocal delivery (other takes sounded far too stoic or sarcastic), it struck the precise balance of longing and exhaustion I had sought. Bringing this track to life took roughly a week—serving as the first major trial of the project.

    Post-Production

    No other song has challenged me quite like this one. In total, I generated around twenty iterations (many were structurally broken and discarded immediately). The combination of spoken-word interludes, corporate satire, and pastoral longing made it difficult for the model to establish equilibrium.

    The final musical take was exceptionally clean. Re-integrating the spoken intro and outro from two sibling generations in Ardour was seamless. Final mastering added pre- and post-roll headroom, engaged the master plugin chain, and normalized loudness to -14 LUFS.
