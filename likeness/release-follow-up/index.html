    Release Follow-Up

    Likeness was released on September 25, 2026.

    The Disambiguation Debacle

    The AI had anticipated it, and reality promptly delivered: across several streaming platforms, no new artist profile was created for my release. Instead, through crude string matching, my album was assigned to an existing artist named Jan Köster (and on one platform, even "Jan Koster"). DistroKid's ingestion form explicitly provides a disambiguation flag for new artist profiles. Whether the distributor transmitted this flag incorrectly in the DDEX payload or the DSPs simply ignored it is impossible for me to verify without internal endpoint logs. At least Spotify and Apple Music were able to handle it.

    This failure is doubly frustrating because, as detailed in Distribution, secondary platforms refuse artists access to their catalog or profile prior to the release date. Having delivered the master assets nearly two months in advance, I could easily have resolved the collision well ahead of launch—yet most DSP architectures explicitly deny indie creators this capability. The result is a fractured release. For my personal monograph, the damage is negligible; for an emerging independent artist relying on momentum and pre-release campaigns, such a bug is devastating.

    Speaking as a software engineer, it remains utterly baffling how mature platform architectures fail to parse and respect a simple binary flag within an ingestion payload.

    The Zenodo Release

    Having published deposits on Zenodo and linked GitHub repositories on numerous occasions, this was the first time the synchronization process took an unusually long time to finalize. The delay was likely attributable to the massive influx of bot traffic Zenodo was actively battling around the release window. Just as DSPs are inundated with AI slop, academic data providers and public repositories are groaning under the weight of aggressive, automated web scrapers. It has become an existential struggle for digital infrastructure. Simultaneously, on a web service I manage professionally, I was forced to filter out roughly one million bot requests per day. The fault lies not with artificial intelligence itself, but with human actors who, driven by commercial greed or gross negligence, push our digital commons to the brink of collapse.

    Algorithmic Neighborhoods

    I was keenly curious to observe where Spotify’s recommendation engine would position the album. Operating with zero prior audience, the platform’s recommendation pipeline was forced to act solely on my personal listening history and the raw acoustic features of the audio. The outcome was revealing.

    Unsurprisingly, Taylor Swift was prominently featured—primarily via The Tortured Poets Department and, most intriguingly, folklore: the long pond studio sessions. The algorithm deliberately reached for the intimate acoustic live tracking rather than the polished studio record. Beyond that, the graph was populated by considerably less ubiquitous female singer-songwriters unfamiliar to me: Megan O’Neill, Gabrielle Aplin, Joy Williams, and Bird of Figment, to name a few.

    I document this not to self-aggrandize or draw unwarranted aesthetic parallels to their work, but because it illustrates the precise niche into which the project navigated. The album operated like a key unlocking obscure, neglected corridors of Spotify's latent graph—what might be termed the deep web of recommendation algorithms. To be sure, this recommendation vector is a one-way street: no listener streaming these artists will organically discover Likeness in their automated radio. Even so, I deeply appreciate the algorithmic company in the depths of the latent graph.
